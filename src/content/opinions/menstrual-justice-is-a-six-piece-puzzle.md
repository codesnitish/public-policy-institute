Menstruation has always been seen as a hygiene issue. However, menstruation is not just limited to hygiene.

The UN places sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) into four categories: sexual, physical, emotional, and deprivation of services and resources. When a menstruator does not get access to a menstrual product due to stigma, that too becomes a form of SGBV. Menstrual discrimination is thus a form of violence.

1.8 billion people across the world menstruate. When a population of this size experiences it, why is there so little discussion on it? Why is access still an issue?

Menstrual health is a human right. Human rights involve an individual living with dignity. Those who do menstruate deserve to do so with safety and dignity. This is where the concept of menstrual justice comes in.

Menstrual justice is a human rights and social justice approach that looks at menstruation through access, safety, health, and dignity. Let us look at each element up close.

In Ethiopia, one in every five females uses menstrual products in urban areas. Females in rural areas are far less likely to use a menstrual product as compared to their counterparts in urban regions. This indicates limited access to menstrual products. In Bangladesh, only 32% of women said they were aware of menstruation before they got their first period. This clearly demonstrates limited information regarding menstruation and menstrual health. A study conducted across 12 countries in rural areas found that one in 10 women did not have a private place to wash themselves. This highlights the issue of safety and dignity.

A major cause of these issues is the stigma and limited systemic approach to cater to the needs of menstruators. Menstrual stigma has thus become a public health issue.

This calls for immediate action towards the issue. The menstrual dignity and justice framework aims to enable gender-diverse people to menstruate without discrimination and stigma. It factors in the intersectional and diverse needs of those menstruating. It aims to ensure access to safe, affordable, and environmentally sustainable menstrual products.

## How Can One Actually Implement These?

This calls for another framework that can be utilised to undertake implementation, an integrated one that develops an ecosystem that reinforces itself at every step. The framework has six pillars: rights and justice, health system, education and awareness, economic empowerment, infrastructure, and social transformation.

Implementation begins with recognition that menstrual health and justice are imperative for the wellbeing of people and therefore must be recognised as a right. Constitutional and legal provisions can strengthen this approach. By utilising formal means of social control, one can push for changes with more vigour. Health systems must incorporate regular checks and gender-diverse experiences while diagnosing and treating patients. Health professionals must be trained and sensitised to gender-diverse needs. Awareness must be created regarding menstruation to debunk myths and counter stigmas and dogmas around it, thus also fuelling the engine of social transformation around the perception of menstruation. Education on menstrual health, best practices, and disorders can lighten the fear around it. Creating spaces where menstruators can wash and clean, and providing access to menstrual products, allows for menstruating with dignity.

It is key to understand that menstrual health and justice is not just limited to individuals in the age range of menstruation, but starts from early childhood nutrition and extends to menopausal care. Menstrual health extends throughout the life course of an individual.

Menstrual justice is the need of the hour. It is about ensuring that 1.8 billion people get to live through their bodily process with safety and dignity. By taking a systematic approach at all elements of the ecosystem and at policy levels, this can be attained. Menstrual literacy through awareness and education can help bring change in perception and slowly transform what menstruation means to society and its inhabitants.
